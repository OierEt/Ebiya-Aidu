How do we plan a holiday?

Peaks, endings and the difference between living a moment and remembering it.

← About me

We usually plan a holiday by counting: how many days, how many places, how much we can fit in. Yet when the trip is over, almost none of that is what we keep. So the real question is not how long to go away or what to see, but what we want to bring back. To answer that, it helps to go back to a much bigger question: what is the meaning of life?

Maybe we could summarize it with the phrase “enjoying the passage of time.” Let’s imagine we’re on a train. The only thing we really know is that the train is moving forward, and we cannot truly question why we’re on that train, because we don’t have an answer (at least not yet). So, the only thing left for us to do is to enjoy the ride. That’s why understanding and internalizing the meaning and implications of that phrase can help us get the most out of life.

Moving to the next point, one of the most logical questions that comes up is: how can we maximize this “enjoying the passage of time”? The answer is deeply subjective. For some people, it might mean spending time with loved ones. For others, grabbing a beer with friends on a random afternoon. In my opinion, and in my case, I think that all of that can be put into or be part of the same bag. Those answers are valid, but I’m searching for something broader and deeper. The label I’d put on that bag would be: creating memories. So ultimately our question becomes: how can we maximize memories?

To answer that, we need to go to a psychological experiment.

In 1993, Daniel Kahneman and Barbara Fredrickson carried out the following study: they gathered 32 participants and told them they were studying the difference in pain perception between the dominant and non-dominant hands. To do this, participants had to place one hand in a bucket of water. After doing that, and after a 15-minute break, they would repeat the process with the other hand. Participants were not told how long they would have to keep their hand in the water. During the trial, they had to rate their discomfort on a scale. To avoid biases, half of the participants randomly started with their dominant hand and the other half with their non-dominant hand. At the end of each trial, they had to give an overall rating for the experience.

But that wasn’t the real goal of the experiment. What participants didn’t know is that the two trials were different. They began the same way: one hand submerged in water at 14°C for 60 seconds, and one of the trials ended there. But the other trial lasted an extra 30 seconds, during which the water warmed slightly to 15°C.

This slight increase reduced discomfort, though it was still unpleasant. Again, to avoid bias, the researchers randomized whether the long trial came first or second, and whether it involved the left or right hand.

The ultimate question was: “If I asked you to repeat one of these two experiences, which would you choose?” Almost 70% of participants preferred the longer trial, the one that had been painful for a longer period. Both trials included the same 60 seconds at 14°C. The longer one simply added 30 seconds of slightly warmer, but still uncomfortable, water. It was 50% longer and yet it was the preferred one.

As strange as it seems, under certain circumstances people prefer more pain over less. Why?

Fredrickson and Kahneman ran a follow-up study with pleasant and unpleasant videos. Participants watched short and long versions of videos like a puppy playing with a flower or waves breaking on a beach, as well as unpleasant ones like footage from Hiroshima or a medical amputation. After watching, they rated the experience. They found that the length of the video had almost no impact on how it was remembered. Kahneman and colleagues suggested that this happens because we experience the world in two ways: in the moment as the experiencing self, and retrospectively as the remembering self.

If you are going through something unpleasant and someone offers to stop it, your experiencing self will probably accept. But for the remembering self, the duration matters very little. This is called duration neglect.

A 2008 study (Kemp et al.) found that the length of a vacation had no effect on how positively it was remembered. In other words, we could say that the duration matters to the experiencing self, but not to the remembering self. So, if duration doesn’t shape memory, what does?

Key moments, especially those that trigger strong emotional responses, good or bad. The funniest, most joyful, most painful, or most frightening moments are the ones we remember, and they come to represent the whole experience. This, makes sense: I don’t remember a random day from when I lived in Madrid. Do I remember what I cooked or what I did on February 10th? No. Why? Because it was an ordinary day with no strong emotional reaction, so no memory was formed or stored. The same happens with vacations: unless you are passionate about churches and unless something unusual happens in that church you visited, you probably won’t remember visiting it at all. This doesn’t mean that visiting churches or doing that kind of tourism is wrong, it's totally valid, and many people enjoy it. But in our case, we’re trying to maximize memory creation. So, unless something extraordinary happens (like the priest falls down the stairs) you won’t remember the visit in the future. And remember, emotional reactions can also be negative: imagine getting your wallet stolen on vacation. That would absolutely stay in your memory.

Now we understand how memories are created and how we can generate them. So, if memories belong to the remembering self, how can we provoke that emotional response? In my opinion, it’s simply about stepping out of your comfort zone. Staying within routine doesn’t create emotional responses because everything falls within what we already expect. No strong emotion means no memory. But when something breaks the pattern, it becomes much more likely that a strong emotional reaction will occur. That’s why stepping outside your comfort zone can cause fear—it’s unfamiliar, and we don’t know what to expect. Fear is natural, but the key is not to let it paralyze us. Once we’re out of our comfort zone, everything is new, and memories are much easier to generate.

So, when planning a holiday, if our goal is to maximize memories, the duration doesn’t matter as much as the unusual or unexpected activities that happen, because those are what we’ll remember. This connects beautifully with the train metaphor, because it’s another way of describing “enjoying the passage of time,” even if it’s subjective. In the end, not only vacations but life itself works this way: the memories we carry are born from key moments, good or bad.

So, we could summarize everything by saying that stepping outside the comfort zone is the main trigger for producing what we can call “peak experiences.”

On top of that, our brains recall and therefore assign more importance to recent events. This is called recency bias. If you tried to list everything you ate last week, you’d remember the most recent meals more easily. That’s why the end of an experience plays such an outsized role in its memory. For example, how many people are rewatching Game of Thrones right now compared to Breaking Bad? The final season of Game of Thrones was so terrible that many people refuse to rewatch the entire show. The ending contaminated the whole memory.

This seems to apply to how we judge the quality of a life as well. In a 2001 study, researchers told participants about Jen: a single woman with no children who lived a very happy life full of friends, vacations, meaningful hobbies, and a job she loved. At age 30, she gets hit by a car and dies instantly. Participants rated how desirable her life was. Another group heard the same story, but with five extra years of life—pleasant, but not as good as the first 30. Surprisingly, people rated the longer life as less desirable. Those extra “good but not great” years worsened the perceived quality of her entire life. They replicated the same result with age 60 vs. 65. Adding mildly pleasant years to a very positive life doesn’t enhance it; it actually diminishes the perceived quality. The ending matters.

So, when holding your hand in cold water, your memory of the experience comes down to two things: the peak and the end. Both trials had the same peak of discomfort, but the longer trial had a softer ending. That slight improvement was enough to trick the remembering self. It was a terrible choice for the experiencing self, but a good one for the remembering self.

This has huge implications. If we remember medical procedures more positively, we’re more likely to repeat them, improving long-term health. Kahneman himself tested this with colonoscopies in 2003: adding three minutes of mild discomfort at the end made patients remember the procedure as less unpleasant and increased the odds that they would return for follow-up screenings.

The peak-end rule also appears in customer experience design: think of the hot dogs at the exit of IKEA. That pleasant final moment softens the whole memory of the shopping trip. A doctor giving a child a lollipop works the same way.

Knowing how our brain creates memories allows us to craft better ones. Want better memories of your vacations? Making them longer won’t help nearly as much as creating novel and exciting days. Make sure there’s a high-peak moment, and end with something nice. In short: to create better and more memories, optimize for the peak and the end and to optimize this, step out of your comfort zone.
